# Arguments for the Phases

**Use only to argue for a phase's value, never as a menu to pick from.**

Read this when a user dismisses a phase as not worth designing — Excitement and Extension get dismissed most — or when they think the framework is too abstract to be worth the effort.

Do not read it because a user is stuck for ideas. Stuck is handled with narrower questions, not with borrowed material.

**Everything here is an effect or a claim, never a mechanism.** That is deliberate. If you find yourself about to describe what someone *did*, you have crossed into designing — describe what it produced instead. The participant quotes are the sharpest tool in the file: they are people describing a phase failing them, in their own words. A user can argue with a quote. They cannot paste one into a run of show.

Cite these as what they are: participant research from Andy Sontag's 5E work, not as your own observation. The quotes are anonymised. Do not attach names to them, and do not invent a name, role, organisation or country for a speaker in order to make a quote land harder.

---

## Excitement

**The claim.** The gap between "yes I'll come" and walking through the door is never empty. Something fills it, and whatever fills it sets what people believe they are walking into.

> When participants know what the gathering is (and what it's not), they show up differently. With clearer expectations, they ask better questions. With ownership, they take more meaningful risks. And with agency, they leave with a stronger sense of purpose and responsibility — not just for what happened, but for what they do next.

The chain worth putting to a skeptic: expectations shape the questions people ask; the questions shape the risks they take; the risks shape what they leave with. A user skipping this phase is not saving effort — they are letting the chain start somewhere they did not choose.

**What a participant said about wanting in earlier:**

> "I want more ways to influence the topics that get all the participants attention."

**If they object that pre-work is homework nobody reads:** they are rejecting a mechanism, not the phase. The material above makes no claim about pre-work. It claims that arrival state is designed or inherited. Concede the mechanism fast and hold the question.

## Entry

**The claim.** How someone is positioned the moment they arrive sets their posture for the rest of the experience — and most designs skip straight to content without deciding what that posture should be.

> When participants know what the gathering is (and what it's not), they show up differently.

That's the second half of the expectations chain used for Excitement. The first half is about what happens before arrival; this half is about arrival itself. Expectations set in advance still have to land somewhere, and Entry is where they either take hold or get overwritten by whatever the room actually does in the first few minutes.

**The sharper version, as a design principle rather than a quote:**

> Frame roles clearly: help people see themselves not as guests or observers, but as co-authors of their own experience.

This is the one to put in front of a user whose Entry is a round of introductions or a restated agenda. Neither of those tells someone what kind of participant they're allowed to be. A round of names answers "who is here." It doesn't answer "am I here to watch or to act."

**A second, independent source for the same idea:**

> Make space sacred: treat creation spaces like rituals, not stations. Let people arrive, settle, and explore.

This comes from material about a different phase entirely, which is worth naming to a skeptical user — the claim that arrival needs room before it needs content shows up in thinking about Engagement too, not only about openings. It isn't a one-off opinion about icebreakers.

**If a user still doubts Entry is worth designing:** it is rarely the phase people dismiss outright — they know they need to open, they just open badly, by defaulting to an agenda or a name-round because nothing else came to mind. That makes Entry as much a Pitfall Radar problem as an argument problem. Lead with the role-framing principle above; it reframes "how do we open" as "who are we telling people they get to be," which is a sharper question than most users have asked themselves.

## Engagement

**The claim.** There is a real difference between interaction and authorship, and most designs stop at the first.

> Creation isn't just an activity, it's a statement of trust. When we invite people to make something during a gathering, we say: "We trust you, and your voice shapes this." This is radically different from "engagement," which often stops at interaction. Creation moves people from witnesses to co-creators.

Making something tangible enhances presence, deepens engagement, and gives the group a shared anchor for meaning. The second claim — that it produces *shared ownership* — is the one to press, because it is what an activity alone does not do.

**The choice argument, which is the same point from the other side:**

> When participants feel that the space honors their autonomy, they're more likely to stay present, go deep, and connect in ways that feel authentic. Choice builds trust. And trust builds transformation.

Curated autonomy, not total freedom. A user who hears "choice" and pictures chaos has heard the wrong claim.

**What participants said:**

> "People sponge, they don't create. I wish gatherings were more about everyone creating."

> "When you're doing something with others, for something larger than yourself — that's where meaning shows up."

> "Those peer-led sessions were some of the most rich… we got exactly what we needed."

The first of those is the most useful thing in this file for a user whose Engagement is mostly presentation. It is a participant naming the failure from inside it.

## Exit

**The claim.** Synthesis is what converts having experienced something into being able to act on it. Skipped, the experience stays undigested — and the cost is invisible at the time, which is why Exit gets compressed when a session runs late.

**What a participant said:**

> "There are so many things back to back that you end up skipping reflection of what you experienced."

Note the passive construction — *you end up skipping*. Nobody decided to cut reflection. It got squeezed. That is the Exit failure mode, and it is worth naming to a user whose Exit is fifteen minutes at the end of a full day.

---

## Extension

**The claim.** This is the best-evidenced phase in the material, and the most dismissed.

> The gathering doesn't end when people walk out the door, unless we let it. What happens after the gathering determines whether the experience takes root or fades away. Without intentional space to align collective goals with participants' own, to deepen relationships, and to build accountability, impact slips through the cracks. Integration isn't a bonus — it's essential to impact and lasting change.

And the sharper framing: **design for transformation, not transmission.** The true measure of learning is not what happens during the programme but what unfolds afterward — people shifting how they see, connect and act. (See Bortoletto & Sontag, *Learning happens when you leave the room*, 2025, for the underlying argument.)

**What participants said:**

> "I don't generally walk away with a clear action that I'm supposed to take."

> "The events have a beginning, middle and end. But for fellows, gatherings are reunions, new collaborations that continue old ones."

The first is the one to use on a user who thinks Extension is optional. It is a participant reporting the exact failure, without blame, as if it were normal — because it is.

The second does something different: it describes what Extension looks like when it has been working long enough to change what an event even *is* to the people attending. Useful against "we don't have the budget for follow-up" — the claim is not about budget, it is about whether each gathering is an isolated capsule or part of a continuing relationship.

## The whole-arc case

A three-day experience design intensive in Amsterdam, 18 professionals, participants working on their own real offerings.

What each phase achieved: participants arrived already believing something better than their current practice was possible; the group shared a lens before any work started, so no time went on negotiating what they were there for; the participants did the designing rather than the facilitators; people left with confidence in their own work rather than comprehension of a model; and the change showed up afterwards in how they designed.

That last point is the whole argument for Extension in one line: **what persisted was a changed practice, not a document.**

The team also departed from the template where the outcome called for it. Useful against "the framework is too rigid" — a user who feels boxed in has usually mistaken the questions for the answers.
