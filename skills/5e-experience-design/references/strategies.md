# Nine Gathering Design Strategies

From *Kaospilot Gathering Design Strategies*, by Andy Sontag and Juliana G., drawing on interviews with practitioners including Miguel Melgarejo, Anamaria Dorgo, Christine Nieves, Peggy Segal, Okong'o Kinyanjui, Eric Dawson, Letícia Schuelter and Hildur Maral. Quotes here are used anonymously; do not attach names.

**How to use this file.** These are lenses for reading a design, not a checklist to run. Two or three per session, chosen because you actually see the signal in front of you. Read the strategy's insight to know what it is watching for, the guiding questions to know what to ask, and the traps to know what to name.

**The traps are the useful half.** A trap is a way a good intention goes wrong, which makes it far more usable than a rule. Most designers are already trying to do the right thing; what they miss is the specific way their version fails.

**"What it produces" is written as effects, and must stay that way.** Each strategy ends with what it looks like across the arc, described by what it achieves rather than what it is. This is deliberate and it is the line that keeps this file usable. The source publication has concrete moves for each strategy, five columns of them, and they are excellent in a publication and poison here: a model reading "set up maker labs to draw, build and reflect" will offer it to someone, and the moment it does that the design stops being theirs. So say what a move accomplishes and let them invent the move. If you find yourself about to name an activity, you have crossed the line, and the sentence needs rewriting as an effect.

**Adding to this file.** New traps are welcome and are the most valuable thing to add, particularly ones seen in real rooms rather than written for a publication. New principles are fine: they are stance rather than instruction. New activities are not.

## 1. The Power of Creation

**The insight.** Inviting participants to make something tangible, whether physical, visual or expressive, enhances presence and deepens engagement. It also produces shared ownership, and gives the group an anchor for meaning.

Creation is not just an activity, it is a statement of trust: we trust you, and your voice shapes this. That is different from "engagement", which often stops at interaction. Creation moves people from witnesses to co-creators.

**Ask:** What could participants make that expresses the purpose of the gathering? How can creation be seeded early, or culminate at the end? What frames would allow creativity rather than constrain it?

**Principles.** Make it playful. Use it as a break from thinking to making, from head to hands. Creation takes risk, so it needs framing and support to get going. Treat creation spaces as rituals rather than stations: let people arrive, settle and explore. Invite, do not assign. And let people take what they made home, which turns it into Extension.

**Traps:**
- Not enough framing or time for participants to get started.
- Over-managing the creation process, stifling spontaneity and intrinsic motivation.
- No time to reflect on or share what they made.
- Tokenising creativity: art-making without meaning.


**What it produces, if a designer asks what this looks like in the arc.** Describe the effect, never the activity. Before people arrive, being asked to bring something of their own changes who they think they are in the room. At Entry, holding a made object rather than a name badge gives people something to do with their hands and their nerves. In the middle, a thing the whole group is building means nobody is only an audience. At the end, work that is looked at rather than just made is the difference between a session and a showcase. And afterwards, what people carry out is the only part of the design that is still working a month later.

## 2. Thriving Inside Out

**The insight.** Better gatherings for participants begin with better gatherings for staff. If you want participants to feel grounded and brave, the team has to lead. Facilitators need to live the transformation they want participants to experience.

Design with a double lens: how does this nourish not only those being served, but those doing the serving?

**Ask:** What are you doing to nourish the people holding the space, before, during and after? Where does the process honour rest, boundaries and team wisdom? How are you modelling the care you want participants to experience?

**Principles.** Care is culture: how the team experiences the gathering shapes how everyone else does. Sustainability starts in the design, through realistic scope, recovery time and peer support, rather than celebration afterwards. Make the labour of care visible and named. Treat boundary-setting by staff as a positive tactic. As above, so below: what you build inside shows up outside.

**Traps:**
- Invisible labour celebrated but not resourced.
- Teams under pressure to do more with less, with no structural change.
- Prioritising external polish over internal health.
- Forgetting the team's emotions because the focus is on production.


**What it produces.** Rhythms agreed before the work starts mean the team is not negotiating their own survival mid-event. Naming what the gathering is and is not, to staff as well as participants, removes the quiet pressure to over-deliver. Making it visible when someone is on or off duty means rest can actually happen rather than being theoretical. Including the team in the closing changes who the gathering was for. And writing down what the team learned about its own limits is what stops the next one costing the same.

## 3. Gathering Experience Ownership

**The insight.** Ownership is a mindset built from the first invitation to the final follow-up. When participants know what a gathering is, and what it is not, they show up differently: clearer expectations produce better questions, ownership produces more meaningful risks, and agency means they leave with responsibility for what happens next.

The aim is participants who help shape the transformation rather than absorbing an event.

**Ask:** How are you shaping expectations before the gathering? How are you actively listening to what participants need, and collecting that for the design? Where in the flow are you reinforcing agency, through language, structure or reflection?

**Principles.** Curiosity over assumptions: ask what people want rather than guessing. Expectations are part of the design, so surface them early rather than leaving them unspoken. Agency starts before the gathering, through pre-engagement that invites authorship. And frame roles clearly, so people see themselves as co-authors rather than guests or observers.

**Traps:**
- Sharing responsibility without properly onboarding new collaborators into the process.
- Designing separate moments that feel like isolated capsules rather than one whole experience.
- Involving participants so much that no space is left for surprise.


**What it produces.** A first email written as "here is how you can shape this" rather than "here is what we made for you" changes what people think they are allowed to do before they have even replied. Tools rather than agendas, prompts and blank spaces rather than filled ones, make participation the default. Sessions led by participants tend to be the ones people name afterwards. Celebrating what participants made, rather than what the organisers presented, tells everyone what the gathering actually values. And follow-up that participants run themselves is the only follow-up that survives the organiser losing interest.

## 4. Emergence

**The insight.** The most memorable experiences arise in the spaces nobody scripted. The question is what structure is *just enough* to let something unexpected unfold.

The principle worth quoting at a designer: **be over-prepared and under-structured.** Emergence is a practice of trust, and it needs strong containers rather than loose ones.

**Ask:** Where are you creating intentional gaps, moments without fixed outcomes? Where is your structure enabling life to happen? Are you prepared to follow what the room needs instead of what the plan says?

**Principles.** Be over-prepared and under-structured. Curiosity over certainty. Read the room's energy as the signal for the next step. Invite more adaptation than control. Have the confidence to step forward or back at the right moment. And support the unknown: unscripted moments need strong emotional containers, so trust the group and yourself to navigate them.

**Traps:**
- Staff or participants unsure how to respond to open-ended moments.
- Too little structure, producing disorientation rather than emergence.
- A gathering packed so full that nothing can emerge.
- Containers not strong enough to hold the unknown.
- Ambiguity blurring the purpose.


**What it produces.** Framing the thing as co-created, and asking for contributions before it starts, means people arrive with something rather than waiting to receive. Physical space that can change purpose lets the day go somewhere the plan did not. An instruction that sets an ambitious goal without prescribing the method is what makes a group inventive rather than compliant. Naming the emergent moments at the end tells people their improvisation counted. And collecting what participants started, rather than only what you planned, is how the next one gets better.

## 5. Generous Exclusion

**The insight.** Saying no can be generous. It protects the integrity of an experience and honours the people who genuinely want to be there. Not every gathering is for everyone, and clarity about that builds trust and allows depth, because people know they are in the right place.

Boundaries become invitations when they are explained with care.

**Ask:** Who is this gathering really for, and are you willing to say so out loud? What does this space need to protect in order to go deep? Are you communicating boundaries in a way that is clear, kind and purposeful?

**Principles.** Quality of participants over quantity. Create a temporary alternative world where everyone's role is clear. Transparent communication as an ally. Clarity as a tool for depth rather than division. Clarify your why and your who, and let purpose guide both the guest list and the design. Say no with kindness, because boundaries become invitations when they are explained with care. Design for depth rather than universality.

**Traps:**
- Using "this isn't for everyone" to dismiss honest feedback and exclude voices.
- Elitism, or a lack of transparency about the basis for selection.
- Avoiding the complexity of inclusion work by narrowing too fast.
- Replacing richness with similarity, producing a simpler and thinner gathering.


**What it produces.** Saying plainly what the gathering is not filters before anyone spends money or a day. Participation framed as a choice rather than an obligation changes what showing up means. Spaces that honour different levels of comfort mean nobody has to perform enthusiasm they do not have. Asking what people chose *not* to do, and why it mattered, is a reflection question most designs never think to ask. And recording who it did not work for, rather than only who it did, is the difference between evaluation and reassurance.

## 6. Build Your Own Adventure

**The insight.** Curated autonomy: offer real choice, then trust participants to choose what is best for them. When a space honours autonomy, people stay present, go deeper and connect more authentically. Choice builds trust, and trust builds transformation.

This is not total freedom. It is clear paths with different rhythms, tones and depths.

**Ask:** Where can you give choice? What kind of choice matters most to this group: pace, format, topic, depth? Are the options clearly framed, or do they create confusion? How does the design balance freedom with support? Can you actually execute every path well?

**Principles.** Design clear choice points, and make them visually obvious. Spend time making sure people are clear about what they want from the gathering, then let them navigate towards it their own way. Design choices that allow deep meaning-making, reaching into identity, beliefs and assumptions. Build narrative through the paths and roles on offer.

**Traps:**
- Participants choosing easy paths and missing the growth.
- A flow so complicated it produces choice fatigue.
- Too much workload for staff to sustain every option.


**What it produces.** Pre-work that arrives before the choices do means people choose with clarity rather than at random. A visible map of the paths available turns navigation into part of the experience. Parallel routes with genuinely different rhythms, reflection against action, movement against stillness, let a group of different people all be in the right room. Multiple ways to reflect at the end means the ones who do not talk still land it. And the group coming back together after going separate ways is what stops choice becoming fragmentation.

## 7. Wellbeing in Focus

**The insight.** Participants increasingly want gatherings that nourish the whole person, not just the work. When participants are thriving they are more effective at the impact they came to create. Not less ambition: deeper alignment. Care is a condition for creativity, because the more people feel safe and seen, the braver they become.

Integrate care into the flow, not into the margins.

**Ask:** Are you creating space for people to check in with themselves, not just with the content? Where does the design invite restoration rather than only stimulation? How is care built into the experience rather than left at the edges?

**Principles.** Design for all nervous systems, considering sensory load, emotional states and energy rhythms in the pacing. Integrate care into the flow rather than the margins. Yes to inner alignment and outer impact, not one at the expense of the other. Deep listening to oneself as central to sustainable change. And care as a condition for creativity: the more people feel safe and seen, the braver they become.

**Traps:**
- Forcing wellbeing onto people.
- So much wellbeing that there is too little substance.
- An individual focus that ignores the systemic causes of the problem.
- Taking participants into deep emotions that cannot be contained.
- Treating wellbeing as separate from the "real" work.


**What it produces.** Asking people to set an intention about rest, before anything else is asked of them, signals what kind of space this is. Opening with grounding rather than logistics changes the first thing the room does together. Wellbeing anchors placed daily rather than once mean it is structural rather than gestural. Normalising stepping out means the people who need it take it. And reflecting on how the process felt, not only what got achieved, is what makes the care legible rather than assumed.

## 8. Make It Last

**The insight.** The gathering does not end when people walk out, unless we let it. What happens afterwards determines whether the experience takes root or fades. Integration is not a bonus, it is essential to impact.

**Design for transformation, not transmission.** The true measure of learning is not what happens during the programme but what unfolds afterwards: people shifting how they see, connect and act. (Bortoletto & Sontag, *Learning happens when you leave the room*, 2025.)

**Ask:** What will help people integrate what they experienced, practically and emotionally? Are you offering structures that support post-event connection and action? How are you closing in a way that opens something else?

**Principles.** Focus on application in the pre-work: schedule peer time after the event, and clarify where people will apply this. Sustainable behaviours over effervescent action, which means having people confront the blockers to application and plan around them. Design for memory and reflection, with time to land what was learned. Build relational scaffolds, because people follow through when they are not doing it alone. And make sure everyone leaves with a clear action and tangible steps for relational accountability.

**Traps:**
- Accountability without connection.
- Follow-up that feels like homework.
- Next steps suggested by the organisers instead of chosen by participants.


**What it produces.** Pre-work aimed at the conditions for application, rather than at the content, means people arrive already thinking about afterwards. Partners assigned from day one give the follow-through somewhere to live. Surfacing the barriers during the event, rather than discovering them later, is what separates a plan from a wish. A closing that harvests rather than summarises changes what people carry. And follow-up on structures the participants chose is the only follow-up that is not homework.

## 9. Intentional Friction

**The insight.** Shared effort builds strong bonds. Facing challenge together, physical, emotional or creative, deepens connection and creates stories that last. This is the oldest narrative form we have.

**Stretch, don't shock.** Friction is never discomfort pushed onto people; it is an invitation into it with safe, supportive frames. Design for consent, and never mistake silence for agreement.

**Ask:** What kind of challenge could deepen connection in this group? How will you frame it so it feels like an invitation rather than a test? What supports people before, during and after the stretch?

**Principles.** Design for consent: always offer an opt-in, and never mistake silence for agreement. Framing matters, because how people are introduced into friction makes an enormous difference. Discomfort fuels transformation, but stretch rather than shock: challenges should be meaningful, ambitious and well supported. And frame the why, so people understand how the discomfort leads to insight or connection.

**Traps:**
- Mistaking hardship for transformation.
- Surprising participants with discomfort and no support.
- Introducing challenge without emotional safety.


**What it produces.** Saying clearly what the gathering asks of people, before they commit, makes the hard parts consented to rather than sprung. Letting people select into deeper layers means the challenge finds the people ready for it. Shared effort towards something that matters is the oldest way humans have of becoming a group. Reflecting on what overcoming it did for people individually and collectively is what turns an ordeal into a story. And smaller participant-run groups afterwards are where the friction keeps paying off.

## A worked example: Offbeat Fest

When someone asks to see a finished 5E design rather than be asked questions about their own, this is where you send them. Showing a completed example is not designing for them. It is showing what the model looks like when someone else has finished using it, on a project that is not theirs and cannot be copied into theirs.

**How to use it.** Read it out as a whole rather than mining it for moves. What is worth pointing at is how the three Meaningful Outcomes surface differently in each phase, and how far apart the ambition at the top and the moves at the bottom sit. Say plainly that it is one design, not a template, and that theirs will look nothing like it. If they start asking for the moves, that is the wrong read: give them the pattern in "What this example is really teaching" below instead.

### The case

Offbeat Fest, a two-day conference for about 115 learning and development professionals at Big Penny Social in London, June 2025. First edition, tight timeline, a two-person team based in Bucharest with ten volunteers, ten roundtable facilitators and seven speakers. Lavinia Mehedințu had been through the Kaospilot Experience Design programme in Copenhagen a year earlier and used the festival to apply the model at scale. Her published handbook, *How We Designed Offbeat Fest*, documents the whole thing, including what did not work.

She names three steps and runs them in this order: define the Meaningful Outcomes, define the Emotional Journey, then design what happens. The agenda is the last thing decided, and it is decided by the two things above it.

### Stage one: the Meaningful Outcomes

The question she asked: if Offbeat Fest could only leave people with three things, what would those be?

**Connection.** People should feel they were not alone in their L&D journeys. That there were others out there thinking deeply, struggling, building.

**Inspiration.** "Not the Pinterest kind. The type that comes from hearing someone else's real story and thinking, maybe I could try that too."

**Feeling good.** "Not just professionally, but as a human. The world is hard. Events can be exhausting. Offbeat Fest should feel like a breath of fresh air."

Then the sentence that makes them real: *"These became our filter. If a decision didn't move us closer to those outcomes, we parked it."*

That is the difference between an outcome and a slogan. An outcome can veto a decision. Note also how much work the negative definitions do: "not the Pinterest kind" carries more than "inspiration" ever could, and it is the kind of line that survives contact with a real planning decision at 11pm.

### Stage two and three, phase by phase

Her method was to name the feeling first, then design against it. *"We asked ourselves: what emotions will carry people through each moment? And how can we support them to move through them with ease?"*

**Excitement.** The feeling she named, in a participant's voice: *"There's a quiet anxiety that comes with registering for a conference where you know no one. Will I belong? Will I be awkward? Will it feel worth it? We wanted to melt that fear before it had the chance to stick."* What she built against it was a pre-event space that read as a welcome rather than an information dump, informal conversation running before anyone arrived, and an optional call for anyone nervous about walking in alone. Most of the work was done by publishing who else would be in the room, with faces and links. Anxiety about a room is mostly anxiety about its anonymity, and it costs almost nothing to fix. Her own verdict on this phase: nothing she would change.

**Entry.** The intention: *"We didn't want the usual awkward shuffle into a conference room. We wanted the arrival itself to feel warm, human, and just a little bit unexpected, the kind of welcome that says, 'You're in the right place.'"* Everything at arrival carried tone rather than logistics. The screens ran lines like *"You belong here. Even if you're not sure why yet"* and *"No one here has all the answers. That's why we're here."* Volunteers were briefed to greet rather than process. Speakers were introduced with a reason for being in the room rather than a set of credentials.

The decision worth the whole case: she had written and rehearsed her opening speech a dozen times, saw a facilitator work three days before the event, and handed him the opening instead, coming on stage only afterwards for the practical details. *"Dropping the opening speech to make space for Rubens' magical start felt like a risk at first, but turned out to be one of the most impactful decisions we made."*

**Engagement.** Two elements carried it. Speakers told the full story of a project, including what did not work, and stayed as participants rather than leaving after their slot. Both of those turned out to matter more than the content. Roundtables ran four times across the two days, with topics drawn from a pre-event survey and each participant's four conversations curated by hand. She also designed the seams: short physical check-ins at the point where the day turns from conversation to presentation, deliberately not icebreakers but *"small moments to help people reconnect with themselves and with the room before transitioning to a different mode."*

The strongest single piece of learning design in the whole event was the Time Capsule: at the end of Day 1 participants found a letter under their chairs from their future self, and reflected in groups on what mattered and what was still unclear; on Day 2 they were to rotate to another table, read what a different group had written, and build on it. Retrieval, elaboration, a day of spacing, and social elaboration on someone else's thinking, all in one move. Her intent: *"a layered reflection, one where ideas could grow across people and time."*

**Exit.** The plan was the second Time Capsule, a few reflections shared aloud, and a table of instant photos taken across the two days for people to take home. The Day 2 capsule was dropped, and what replaced it was improvised: everyone named a wish for the future of L&D and a word for their mood, and the room screamed the words together. She calls it *"a big, beautiful mess"* that *"offered some kind of ending"*, and then says the honest thing: *"we left feeling like the experience could have been landed a bit better."*

**Extension.** The plan was photos, presentations and quotes captured on the day, then an invitation into their ongoing membership so the three outcomes carried on past the event. The quote capture was forgotten in the busyness. What actually became the strongest Extension artefact was unplanned: the handbook itself, published so *"others peek behind the scenes. Because if Offbeat Fest was about honest conversations, we wanted our wrap-up to reflect that too."*

### What this example is really teaching

**The design quality tracks the emotional specificity.** Excitement and Entry are the two phases where she wrote down the actual feeling in the actual words of a nervous participant. They are also the two phases that held under pressure. Exit was described in activities and never in what someone should feel walking out of the door, and Exit is where the design collapsed the moment its main activity was cut. This is the cleanest argument anywhere for naming the emotion before designing the moment, and it comes from a real event rather than a principle.

**Everything that failed, failed to fatigue or forgetting, not to a bad idea.** The Day 2 check-in, the second Time Capsule, the quote capture, the photos, a poster left in Bucharest because it was awkward to carry. Every one was sound design defeated by an over-packed agenda, a missing person, or a suitcase. Good design and bad placement are different problems, and designers routinely collapse them. A question worth handing to someone: which of your five phases currently depends on somebody remembering something at the end of a fourteen-hour day?

**Outcomes only count if they can veto.** Ask them to name a decision their outcomes have actually killed. If none, the outcomes are decoration.

**Excitement is cheap and undervalued.** It was the least expensive phase and the only one she would not change a thing about.

**Entry sets identity, not information.** Tone on the screens, mood in the opening, stories in the introductions, and a willingness to bin her own rehearsed speech at 72 hours' notice because someone else could set the tone better.

**Honesty was a design material.** Speakers were asked what did not work. Participants called it refreshing. The handbook does the same thing to the event. There is a straight line from the Meaningful Outcome of connection to the choice to be publicly unfinished.
