# 5E Experience Design

**Design the feelings before the activities.**

Most session planning starts with the agenda. This starts one rung higher: what you would most like to create, what has to change from where people are now, and what they should be *feeling* at each point in the arc. Only then does it ask what has to happen to produce that.

It works through questions, so the design stays yours, and it always ends in a 5E Experience Design Journey Map you can put in front of a client.

Built on the **5E Experience Design Model**, created by [Andy Sontag](https://andysontag.com/5e-model?utm_source=5e-skill&utm_medium=readme&utm_campaign=5e-experience-design) and taught at Kaospilot to designers at Adidas, IKEA, LEGO and Netflix, and to teams running everything from leadership programmes to onboarding to retail.

## The one thing that makes it different

**It will not design for you.**

Ask it for an opening exercise and it will decline, then ask you a better question instead. That is deliberate, and it is the whole point.

A design you generated is one you can defend in the room and still recognise six weeks later. A design an AI handed you is one you have to evaluate, and you will not remember why any of it is there. Most AI planning tools optimise for the feeling of progress. This one optimises for the design being yours.

If you want something to fill in five boxes for you, this is the wrong tool and you will find it annoying.

## How it works

Three stages, always in this order, most abstract first. A designer who starts from "three hours and nine people" designs something that fits three hours and nine people. A designer who starts from what they actually want to happen designs something better and *then* fits it.

1. **Meaningful Outcomes**, what the experience is for, worked down from the ambition rather than up from the agenda
2. **Emotions**, what people should feel at each phase, written as "I feel…" statements before any activity exists
3. **What Happens**, the moves that produce those feelings

Constraints come last, as the thing the design has to survive rather than the frame it gets built inside.

Then the five phases get worked and mapped:

| Phase | The question it makes you answer |
|-------|----------------------------------|
| **Excitement** | Why would anyone care enough to show up fully? |
| **Entry** | How do people actually arrive, mentally and socially? |
| **Engagement** | Where is the real work, and who is doing it? |
| **Exit** | How do people make meaning of what happened? |
| **Extension** | What is still true a month later? |

Nine **Gathering Design Strategies** are available as lenses, each with the traps that go with it, offered as possibly relevant rather than as a diagnosis of your design. A **worked example**, the design behind Offbeat Fest 2025, is there for when you would rather see a finished arc than answer more questions. A **Spar mode** attacks a design you have already committed to, on request.

## What you get

A self-contained HTML **5E Experience Design Journey Map**: the ambition and the core shift, the Meaningful Outcomes, the emotional journey drawn as a curve running under an arc across all five phases, the "I feel…" statement and chosen moves for each phase, and the gaps left visibly open rather than invented.

The Meaningful Outcomes come from Nathan Shedroff's work on core meanings, in *Making Meaning* (Diller, Shedroff & Rhea); the 5E model builds on them.

## Where the model comes from

The full explanation of the 5E model, the research behind each phase, and a free Miro template are at **[andysontag.com/5e-model](https://andysontag.com/5e-model?utm_source=5e-skill&utm_medium=readme&utm_campaign=5e-experience-design)**.

This skill carries the questions. It deliberately does not carry the full diagnostic material or the parts of the method that only work with a live facilitator and a group of peers. If you hit the edge of what the questions here can do, that edge is real, and the page above is where to go next.
